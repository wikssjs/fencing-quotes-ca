Fencing options and costs in Campbell River

In Campbell River, the right fence option usually comes down to balancing privacy and curb appeal against the realities of coastal wind and moderate frost. With 35,519 residents and 11,165 homeowner households (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), there’s steady year-round demand because many single-detached properties—60.8% of dwellings—need boundary upgrades as neighborhoods mature. Campbell River also has a large housing stock built before 1981 (40.0%), which often means older boundary fences are being replaced after long service lives, especially where posts have started to lean or rails have loosened.

On Vancouver Island and the Coast, the frost line is relatively shallow—commonly estimated around 45–60 cm—but posts still need to be set below frost depth to reduce heaving and long-term movement. That typically pushes post holes deeper and increases labour, particularly on wet or clay-rich ground. Wind off the Strait of Georgia and Juan de Fuca Strait can be a major design driver: solid privacy fences act like sails, so taller builds may need heavier posts and closer spacing, while open styles like chain-link tolerate exposure with more standard spacing. Local access constraints—rock pockets, slopes, and narrow driveways near areas like Willow Point—can add time for excavation, alignment, and disposal.

What affects the price of fencing in Campbell River

In Campbell River and across Vancouver Island and the Coast, it’s common to see fencing quotes vary by 30–50% for what looks like the “same” job. The biggest reason is that real sites aren’t uniform: fence lines run into slopes, rock pockets, soft/wet ground, and existing fences that complicate excavation and disposal. Another driver is post design—especially because coastal frost is shallow, yet persistent enough to influence how deep posts must be set to prevent heaving and leaning. Even when the frost line is only about 45–60 cm, the contractor still typically sets posts roughly 4–6 inches below the design frost depth, which often works out to 75–90 cm in typical residential conditions.

Compared to regions with deeper freezes, Ontario and Alberta jobs often need posts 1.5–2.0 m into the ground, and that’s where heavy concrete setting and longer posts can push material and labour significantly higher. On Vancouver Island, we generally need less depth than the Prairies or the interior, but we still pay attention to wet soils: clay-rich areas or saturating rains can increase excavation time and make concrete footings more practical for heavier panels and multi-gate layouts. Wind exposure also matters. Solid privacy fences can require tighter post spacing and more robust framing to prevent flexing—raising labour—while open styles like chain-link tend to hold shape with standard spacing.

Two local examples: (1) replacing an old, leaning fence often adds cost for removal, hauling, and re-setting the line so it’s plumb—particularly near older properties in areas like Rock Bay or Lincoln—then (2) building a tall backyard privacy run on a sloped yard can require stepped/raked sections that add measuring and material waste. If one quote is building a vinyl privacy fence (often in the $70–$110/linear foot band) with concrete-set posts, and another uses lighter post spacing, the differences show up quickly in the total.

Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, fences can trigger permit requirements depending on location, height, and municipal rules. As a practical guideline, fences above a height threshold—often around 2.0 m (6.5 ft) at the rear and about 1.0–1.2 m (3–4 ft) at the front or corner—may require a building permit. Regardless of permit needs, you must keep the fence on your property and respect municipal setback bylaws that apply to your street frontage and corner lots. Because Campbell River sits on active coastal ground where post holes can be deep, planning your layout before you dig is especially important.

Before any post hole is dug, call for utility locates (in B.C., use BC 1 Call). If you don’t already have property pins located and verified, obtain a survey or a property line locate so you’re not building across a boundary—common with older fences that slowly drift over time. Shared fence ownership is governed by provincial line-fences legislation, but the practical takeaway is that you should confirm who owns which side and document agreements in writing if the fence is shared.

To verify a contractor before work starts in Campbell River, check three things: (1) British Columbia licence status (and any required registration) via the relevant online registry listings; (2) certificate of insurance showing general liability suitable for the job; and (3) clearance/coverage proof for workers, where applicable for your contractor’s labour. Ask for the latest certificate numbers and clear, current dates—then match them to the name on the quote and the company that will install your fence.

Choosing the right fence material for Campbell River

Choosing fence material in Campbell River is mostly about matching your priorities—privacy, low maintenance, budget, and lifespan—to local climate realities. Pressure-treated pine is the budget-friendly option, but it needs regular sealing or staining (typically every 2–3 years) to prevent grey weathering and to reduce moisture-driven deterioration. Cedar offers better natural rot resistance and an attractive finish, but it still benefits from sealing to keep it looking good and to slow surface checking after wet winters. Vinyl/PVC is the low-maintenance favourite for many homeowners because it doesn’t require staining; however, very extreme cold snaps can make it more susceptible to impact cracking, so post spacing and hardware details still matter.

Chain-link is usually the lowest cost and can last a long time in coastal conditions, but it provides little privacy unless you add privacy slats or vegetation. Aluminum ornamental fences resist rust well, look sharp, and typically require minimal upkeep, but they don’t provide the same privacy as solid-panel designs. On Vancouver Island, moderate coastal frost means posts are commonly set below frost depth; concrete-set posts are often worthwhile for heavier fences, taller runs exposed to wind, gates, or yards with wet/clay soils, even if the frost depth itself is not as deep as in interior Canada. With wind off the Strait of Georgia, solid privacy fences may also need closer post spacing to reduce flexing and panel “sail” effects, which can shift the price more than material choice alone.

A concrete example: if your privacy goal is similar, a treated pine privacy fence may land in the $55–$85/linear foot range, while cedar or vinyl typically costs more (often $70–$110/linear foot depending on design). That difference is justified when you want fewer maintenance cycles (vinyl) or better appearance and longer service life (cedar), especially for homeowners who don’t want to restain after every couple of wet seasons.

How to choose a fencing contractor in Campbell River

Choosing a fencing contractor in Campbell River is easier when you verify the fundamentals before you ever schedule digging. Start with British Columbia licensing: ask the contractor to provide their current licence details and confirm the business is in good standing where applicable. Next, request a certificate of insurance showing general liability for the project and ensure it matches the legal company name on your quote. For worker coverage, ask for proof of workers’ compensation clearance/coverage (where applicable), so you’re not exposed if an installer is injured on your property.

Then get 2–3 itemised written quotes. Look for a breakdown that separates labour from materials, includes gate hardware, and lists post spacing and post setting method (gravel pack vs concrete-set). A lump-sum only quote makes it harder to compare apples to apples. Also read the exclusions: is old fence removal included? Is disposal/dump fees included? Are site restoration items (topsoil, grading touch-ups, and landscaping protection) part of the scope or treated as add-ons?

Warranty matters too. Ask for both a workmanship warranty (how long the company stands behind installation) and a manufacturer/product warranty (for posts, panels, and rails). Confirm whether the warranty is transferable if you sell your home.

For payment, never agree to pay more than 10–15% upfront. Hold back the remainder until the job is complete, inspected, and cleaned up. Finally, require a timeline in writing: a proposed start date and an estimated completion window.

Common red flags I see in Campbell River: quotes that don’t mention post depth or how they prevent frost heave; “no warranty” or vague warranty wording; missing insurance/coverage paperwork or a reluctance to provide certificate dates; and a poorly defined scope that leaves disposal, line locating, or gate reinforcement as surprise add-ons after excavation.

Frequently asked questions — fencing in Campbell River

Wood vs vinyl vs aluminum fence — which is best?

For Campbell River homes, “best” depends on how much maintenance you want and how exposed your fence line is to coastal wind and wet freeze cycles. Wood (pressure-treated or cedar) can be a strong value, especially when installed with proper post depth for moderate coastal frost, but it typically needs sealing or staining to stay looking sharp. Vinyl/PVC is often the easiest long-term option because it’s low-maintenance—no staining—though it costs more up front, commonly around the $70–$110 per linear foot band. Aluminum ornamental is great for rust resistance and curb appeal, but it doesn’t deliver privacy like a solid wood or vinyl panel. If your goal is privacy, vinyl or cedar usually wins; if you want security with airflow, chain-link is often more cost-effective.

Do I need a permit to build a fence in British Columbia?

In British Columbia, fences may require a building permit if they exceed certain height thresholds, often around 2.0 m for rear fencing and about 1.0–1.2 m for front or corner areas (depending on municipal rules). Even when a permit isn’t required, the fence still must follow property line requirements and municipal setback bylaws. In Campbell River, it’s smart to ask your contractor to confirm whether your planned height/location triggers permitting before excavation—so you don’t end up redesigning after work begins. Regardless of permits, you should call BC 1 Call before digging to protect utilities, and get a survey/locate when property pins aren’t clearly known. If you’re building near a corner or high-visibility frontage, plan for extra checks.

How long does a wood fence last in Campbell River?

With proper installation and maintenance, a wood fence in Campbell River typically lasts about 10–15 years for pressure-treated pine and 15–25 years for cedar. Coastal rain and moderate frost mean post stability is critical: posts should be set below design frost depth (often approximately 75–90 cm in typical residential scenarios) to reduce heaving and leaning over time. Maintenance also affects lifespan. Pine usually needs sealing/staining every 2–3 years to limit weathering, while cedar benefits from periodic sealing (often around every 3 years). If you have wind-loaded privacy panels, stronger framing and appropriate post spacing help prevent sagging, which extends fence life.

Who owns and maintains a shared fence?

Shared fence ownership and responsibilities are addressed under provincial line-fences legislation, but the practical outcome is that you and your neighbour should clarify ownership of each side, maintenance obligations, and who pays for repairs. In Campbell River, older fences sometimes drift onto/near boundaries, so the first step should be confirming the property line with a survey or locate. If the fence is truly shared, maintenance can be split, but disputes usually happen when one party upgrades materials or height without agreement. Before any work, document your plan in writing: the intended fence type, height, material choice, and cost-sharing approach. If you’re replacing a compromised section, keep photos and measurements so your scope is clear and fair.

How do I find my property line before fencing?

The safest way in Campbell River is to get a survey or a professional property line locate before installing posts. If you have property pins/markers, confirm their exact location relative to your fence line—don’t rely on a decades-old fence as proof of boundary, since it may have shifted due to frost movement, wind, or settling. A locate helps avoid encroachment disputes and ensures your contractor is digging in the right spot. Also call BC 1 Call before digging to locate buried utilities. If you’re in a neighbourhood with older housing stock (many homes built before 1981), it’s even more important to verify boundaries because older boundary markers can be missing or inconsistent. Ask your contractor how they plan to measure and set the line.

What's the most low-maintenance fence?

In Campbell River, the lowest-maintenance options are usually vinyl/PVC and aluminum (and many composite systems). Vinyl is popular because it generally doesn’t require staining; most upkeep is an occasional rinse and inspection for impact damage. Aluminum ornamental is also low-maintenance and won’t rust the way untreated steel hardware can, and it typically handles coastal damp very well. These options cost more up front than standard treated wood or chain-link; for example, vinyl commonly falls in the $70–$110 per linear foot range, while chain-link can be $35–$60 if you’re willing to accept low privacy. The “best low-maintenance” choice still depends on your privacy needs: vinyl/composite for privacy, aluminum for a clean front-yard look, and chain-link if security is the priority rather than screening.
