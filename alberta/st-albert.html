Fencing options and costs in St. Albert

St. Albert homeowners often ask the same question: which fence will look good, hold up to Alberta weather, and land in a sensible budget? With about 21,505 homeowner households (82.9% of all households) and single-detached houses making up 70.4% of local dwellings, there’s steady demand for backyard privacy fences, side yard boundary fencing, and replacement work as older runs wear out (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census). That demand is especially visible in family-oriented areas like Glenwood, where properties are spaced for driveways and gates that get used daily—so contractors see a lot of requests for upgrades to privacy and wind-stress resistance.

In the Edmonton economic region, fencing costs are shaped less by material alone and more by site conditions: frost depth means posts generally need to be set below the design frost line to reduce heaving, and that often increases labour time for drilling and concrete setting. Prairies wind exposure also pushes installers toward sturdier post spacing, better hardware, and more rigid panel construction—particularly for taller privacy fences. Access (narrow side gates, landscaping beds, and snow storage) and whether you’re removing an old fence can swing the total project price meaningfully.

What affects the price of fencing in St. Albert

In St. Albert, two neighbours can receive quotes for the “same” fence that differ by 30–50% because the real cost drivers are site and construction details: post depth and setting method, wind-stress panel strength, gate complexity, and how difficult the access is for materials. Even in the same Edmonton-area market, crews may price differently depending on how much concrete drilling, hauling, and disposal they expect. That’s why you’ll see wood fencing land around the $3,000–$6,500 wood band in one yard and climb toward the higher end when labour is heavy, while vinyl/PVC often sits in the $3,500–$8,000 range when privacy height, post rigidity, and gate installs are included.

On the frost side, Edmonton-area installs must account for heaving risk. As a practical rule, posts are typically set 4–6 inches below the design frost depth, which usually means setting depth that works out to roughly 1.5–2.0 m into the ground for many Alberta yards. Deeper holes mean longer posts, more excavation, and more concrete when crews use concrete-set posts. On the Prairies, wind exposure also matters: more exposed sites push contractors to tighter post spacing and heavier rails, especially for privacy fences that catch more wind.

In St. Albert specifically, costs often rise when (1) your yard has a slope that requires stepped or raked panels so the top line stays level, (2) there are mature trees or landscaping beds that limit access for augers, and (3) the existing fence needs removal and proper disposal before the new posts go in. Older housing stock can also affect what you uncover: with 35.0% of homes built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), older properties sometimes have buried lines, irregular boundary markers, or reused posts that slow down the layout and demo process. If your run is straight, fully accessible, and survey pins are easy to locate, the project often trims toward the lower end of the relevant material band.

Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, fence permissions can depend on height and location. A common trigger is fences above about 2.0 m (6.5 ft) for the rear area and 1.0–1.2 m (3–4 ft) near the front or corners—those taller builds often require a building permit, and municipal rules still apply to ensure the fence sits on the correct property line and respects any setback requirements. Even where a permit isn’t required, you still need to plan around property-line compliance and any approved sightline or corner constraints.

Before any post holes are dug, you should arrange utility locates through Alberta One-Call (to protect underground services). You’ll also want a survey or at least a locate of property pins before installing posts so you’re not guessing boundary lines. Shared fence ownership is typically handled under Alberta’s line-fences framework, so clarify responsibilities if you’re replacing or building near a neighbour’s side.

To verify a contractor for your St. Albert project, follow a quick checklist: (1) confirm the contractor’s Alberta business/licence status using the appropriate online registry; (2) ask for an up-to-date certificate of insurance showing general liability (and request the coverage details match your project value and fence type); (3) ensure they carry WSIB/WCB coverage for the workers on site; and (4) request a written clearance letter where applicable. A legitimate fencing crew will provide documents promptly and match the paperwork to the quote scope.

Choosing the right fence material for St. Albert

Choosing fencing in St. Albert is really about matching material to your priorities: privacy, low maintenance, budget, and lifespan—then confirming the design accounts for Alberta frost and Prairies wind. Pressure-treated pine is typically the budget-friendly choice, but it needs staining or sealing every 2–3 years to prevent greying and surface weathering. Cedar offers better natural rot resistance and a warm look, though you still need maintenance to keep it looking clean and to reduce warping at edges. Vinyl/PVC is a popular low-maintenance option because it doesn’t require staining, but extreme cold can affect how panels expand and contract—so the install details (proper spacing and fastening) matter. Chain-link is usually the least expensive and long-lived, but it doesn’t provide privacy unless you add slats or a privacy screen. Aluminum provides a rust-resistant ornamental option, yet it’s typically open for visibility. For homeowners wanting a blend of low maintenance and privacy, composite/recycled boards can be a strong option, provided your local installer uses proper post-depth and fastening strategy.

Because Edmonton-area frost depth can require post placement roughly 4–6 inches below design frost and often around 1.5–2.0 m of embedment, concrete-set posts are commonly used for stability in frost-prone soils—especially for tall privacy fences. Wind and snow load can also stress wider fence sections, so contractors may tighten post spacing and use heavier rails. For budgeting, you might find pressure-treated wood in the $3,000–$6,500 band, while vinyl/PVC privacy often lands in the $3,500–$8,000 band. A realistic example: if your privacy fence needs two gates and sits on a wind-exposed corner lot, the extra rigidity and hardware that pushes a vinyl quote toward the higher end can be justified—because replacing a wind-damaged panel in winter is far more disruptive than the upfront cost.

How to choose a fencing contractor in St. Albert

When you’re comparing fencing contractors in St. Albert, don’t rely on price alone—verify that the crew can legally and safely perform the work in Alberta. Start with licensing and business legitimacy (use the relevant online registry to confirm the business name and status). Next, ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability—then check that the coverage is current and that the policy is suitable for fence installation work (site excavation, post setting, and material handling). For work on your property, confirm WSIB/WCB coverage for workers; many reputable contractors can provide a clearance letter and proof of active coverage on request.

Get 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labour, materials, excavation, post setting method (concrete vs. gravel pack vs. driven), gate hardware, and disposal. A lump-sum quote with no detail is harder to compare if one contractor is allowing different post depth, different spacing, or different gate framing. Read the scope carefully: what’s excluded (landscaping restoration, overgrown access, snow removal for starting), is permit pulling included (when required), and is old fence removal/disposal part of the price?

Also review warranty terms: insist on a clear workmanship warranty (and confirm length in writing), and distinguish it from the product/manufacturer warranty. If you might sell the home, ask whether warranties are transferable. Finally, payment should be sensible—generally never more than 10–15% upfront. Holdback until the job is complete and inspected. A clear timeline matters too: ask for a start date and an estimated completion window in writing.

Red flags I often see with less reliable fencing contractors in the St. Albert area include: vague quotes that don’t specify post embedment depth or concrete setting; promises of “same-day” installs without soil drilling/excavation planning; refusing to provide insurance/WSIB/WCB documentation; payment requests for large upfront deposits; and workmanship warranties that are only verbal or have unclear coverage terms.

Frequently asked questions — fencing in St. Albert

What's the most low-maintenance fence?

In most St. Albert yards, vinyl/PVC is the go-to low-maintenance choice because it won’t require regular staining like wood and it resists rot in freeze-thaw cycles. If you want privacy, select a solid-panel style rather than a more open picket design. Cost-wise, vinyl projects commonly fall within the $3,500–$8,000 installed band, especially once you include proper post embedment for frost protection and any gate hardware upgrades. Chain-link can also be low maintenance, but it offers limited privacy unless you add slats or a privacy screen. The “lowest maintenance” result depends on good installation—especially correct fastening and spacing in cold weather—so ask your contractor how they handle winter expansion and panel alignment.

How tall can my fence be in St. Albert?

Fence height rules in Alberta typically depend on where the fence sits (rear yard vs front or corner areas) and whether the build triggers a permit requirement. A common threshold used in Edmonton-area permitting discussions is around 2.0 m (6.5 ft) for rear areas and 1.0–1.2 m (3–4 ft) for front or corner locations. If you plan to exceed those thresholds, a building permit is often required. In St. Albert, you must also respect property lines and any setback/sightline bylaws that apply to your lot. The safest approach is to ask your fencing contractor to confirm the exact height and location against the permit trigger before ordering materials—especially if you’re building a privacy fence that looks like a “full height” backyard wall.

Should fence posts be set in concrete here?

Often, yes—at least for many privacy fence builds in the Edmonton economic region, including St. Albert—because posts must be set below the design frost depth to reduce heaving. Contractors commonly embed posts roughly 4–6 inches below design frost, and in practical terms that can mean around 1.5–2.0 m of embedment. Concrete setting can provide stability in frost-prone soils and helps reduce movement over freeze-thaw seasons. That said, the “right” method depends on your soil conditions, fence height, and wind exposure. A well-prepared quote should specify the post setting method (concrete vs gravel pack vs driven), the hole depth, and the hardware. If a contractor skips frost-depth planning to cut cost, it may lower the short-term price but can raise long-term repair risk.

What's the best fence for privacy?

For true privacy in St. Albert, you’ll usually get the best results with solid board or solid panel$3,000–$6,500 band, while vinyl privacy commonly sits in the $3,500–$8,000 band depending on gate count and post rigidity. If privacy is your top priority, don’t choose the most open style—confirm the panel type in the quote.

How do I compare fencing quotes?

Compare quotes like a contractor would: look for what’s included, not just the total. First, confirm the linear footage, number and type of gates (walk-through vs drive-through/double), and the post plan (depth and setting method). Second, check the material spec—pressure-treated vs cedar, vinyl thickness/style, and whether chain-link includes tensioning and any privacy slats. Third, verify whether removal/disposal of the old fence is included and whether permit pulling is covered when required. Fourth, ask for an itemised breakdown of labour and materials so differences in cost don’t hide in “miscellaneous.” Finally, review warranties for workmanship and product coverage. If two quotes differ by 30–50%, it’s usually because one is addressing frost-depth, wind stiffness, or access work more thoroughly.

How long does fence installation take in St. Albert?

Typical install timelines in St. Albert depend on length, gate count, and site access. A straightforward replacement or short run can be completed in a day or two for many crews, while longer privacy fences with multiple gates and slope adjustments commonly take several days. Soil conditions and frost considerations matter: post holes and concrete setting (when used) require time and proper curing steps. Weather in Alberta can also affect access and excavation, particularly when the ground is hard or conditions are unsafe. When requesting your start date, ask the contractor to provide a completion estimate in writing and to explain any prerequisites (utility locates, survey locate, removing old fencing). If you’re budgeting, timeline impacts labour—so rushing or cutting scope can be costly later.
