Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, fence rules depend on location and height. As a practical baseline, fences above a height threshold—often around 2.0 m (6.5 ft) at the rear and 1.0–1.2 m (3–4 ft) at the front or corner—can require a building permit. Your municipality may also apply setback and sightline limits, so it’s important not to assume “whatever height I want” is automatically allowed.

Before digging any post hole, call for utility locates through Alberta One-Call to avoid underground service conflicts. Also, get a survey or at least a formal locate of your property pins before installing. This reduces the chance of encroaching on a neighbour’s lot or crossing a utility easement. For shared fences, Alberta’s line-fences framework governs responsibilities—what each party can maintain and who pays for repairs varies by situation.

What typically DOES require a permit: tall rear/privacy fences above the common threshold, substantial structures attached to buildings, and projects where height combined with corner placement triggers local review. What typically does NOT require a permit: modest-height decorative or open fences under the common thresholds, assuming they stay within setbacks and respect property lines.

To verify a contractor before you sign in Sexsmith: check their Alberta licence (if applicable to their scope), request a certificate of liability insurance showing adequate coverage limits for fencing work, and confirm WSIB/WCB coverage documentation. Ask for a clearance letter or current account status; reputable contractors will provide it without pressure. Then compare quotes only after the paperwork matches.

Choosing the right fence material for Sexsmith

The best fence in Sexsmith is the one that matches your priorities while surviving Alberta’s cold snaps, frost heave risks, and prairie winds. Pressure-treated pine is usually the budget-friendly path, but it needs staining or sealing every 2–3 years to limit grey weathering and to keep the surface protected. Cedar looks better longer and resists rot naturally, but it still benefits from periodic maintenance to preserve colour and prevent surface checking. Vinyl/PVC is popular for low upkeep, but in extreme cold you want proper installation with correct fasteners and movement allowances—otherwise panels and components can be more prone to cracking at stress points.

For privacy, wood (pine or cedar) and solid composite boards tend to score highest. Chain-link is the lowest upfront option and can last a long time, but it won’t block views unless you add privacy slats or planters. Aluminum ornamental gives a neat, rust-resistant look with mid-range cost, but it’s inherently more open. In exposed yards, a “more open” style can be less vulnerable to racking, while a full privacy panel needs stronger bracing and closer post spacing.

Because frost commonly runs deep (often 4–6 feet), post-depth and footing design matter as much as the material. Even with premium materials, skipping proper deep setting (frequently concrete-set posts for long-term plumb) can lead to leaning after freeze–thaw cycles. If your soil is rocky or clay-heavy, expect deeper augering time and more concrete—this can shift the job toward the higher end of wood or vinyl bands.

Concrete value example: if vinyl/PVC is priced around $40–$80 per linear foot and cedar/wood privacy runs closer to $50–$85, the extra cost can be justified when you need a long-term “set it and forget it” look, fewer maintenance trips, and a fence designed for wind loading on your exposed side yard.

How to choose a fencing contractor in Sexsmith

Choosing the right fencing contractor in Sexsmith is less about “who’s cheapest” and more about who will build the fence to survive our deep frost and wind. Start with Alberta compliance. Ask for proof of liability insurance (certificate of insurance naming you/your property as appropriate if required by the contract), and confirm WSIB/WCB coverage with documentation showing their current standing. If a contractor won’t provide certificates or clearance letters, treat that as a serious warning—especially on deep post installs where equipment damage or jobsite injuries are higher-risk.

Next, request 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labour and materials (posts, rails, mesh/panels, brackets, hardware) and clarifies how posts will be set (concrete vs. gravel pack). Avoid lump-sum estimates without scope notes. Read exclusions carefully: Is old fence removal included? Is haul-off/disposal included? Is your locate/survey work included or “by homeowner”? A good contractor will also state whether permits are handled when needed and who pays.

For warranty, confirm two parts: workmanship warranty length (how long they stand behind installation) and product/manufacturer warranty on materials. Ask if the warranty is transferable if you sell the home. Regarding payment, never agree to more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until the job is complete and measured. Finally, get a start date and completion estimate in writing so winter access delays are planned for rather than disputed later.
